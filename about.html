Designer who codes, scientist who builds

I design and build tools that help people understand each other better. My background in neuroscience and psychology taught me how humans work. My startup experience taught me how to ship products. And my comedy hobby taught me that if you can't explain something simply, you don't understand it yet.

Currently finishing my MSc in Psychology at Birkbeck while building Pont, an AI-powered platform that helps people find therapists they'll actually click with. Before that: gene drives at Imperial, brain organoids at MRC-LMB, and a failed-but-educational gene testing startup that taught me the difference between cool science and useful products.

How I got here

2025 - Building Pont: From therapist matching to friendship compatibility

Started with a problem I'd been living: How do you find the right therapist? I've spent most of my life trying to systematize human interaction because otherwise I feel like an alien. So I built a prototype where two GPT agents with pre-defined personalities talked to each other—essentially simulating therapeutic conversations to predict compatibility.

It was fun but expensive as hell. So I turned it into something real: combined social simulation principles with recommender systems, built a full-stack Flask app with TypeScript, designed the entire interface from scratch (user flows, matching algorithm visualization, onboarding sequences), and integrated Stripe for booking. Conducted 15+ clinician interviews to refine the matching algorithm and improve accessibility.

When therapists said it was too risky to adopt early-stage, I pivoted the core compatibility engine into a friend-matching platform. Same fundamental challenge—predicting human connection—different use case. Currently iterating based on user feedback.

What I learned: How to design developer-facing features (API documentation, configuration interfaces), how to balance technical complexity with user simplicity, and that the best research happens when you're solving your own problem.

2025 - Learning to design for neurodivergent users

Started volunteering with Spectrum Socials, helping run friendship-building sessions for autistic kids (ages 5-16). I'm essentially event coordinator and conversation moderator—designing social experiences that help kids learn interaction patterns in a safe environment.

Loneliness is a massive theme here. Watching real connections form between kids who struggle socially has fundamentally changed how I think about interface design. When you're designing for people who interpret the world differently, every micro-interaction matters. Every transition needs to be predictable. Every error message needs to be kind.

This experience directly influences how I approach UX: clear feedback loops, reduced cognitive load, multiple pathways to the same outcome. Universal design isn't just accessible design—it's better design for everyone.

2024 - nGENEsis Diagnostics: When the business model breaks the ethics

Applied solo to Y Combinator with a gene network psychiatry idea—made it to the top 10%. The concept: use whole-genome sequencing to analyze complete gene networks for psychiatric treatment optimization. Did hours of user interviews with clinicians and people at gene-testing companies.

Here's what killed it: The tests people actually need are expensive and hard to access. The "affordable" ones subsidize costs by selling user data. The only model that penciled out was to either sell patient data or charge obscene fees. The business model was fundamentally about hiking up the price of data rather than helping people.

Hard pass. I'm parking it until whole-genome sequencing is cheap and boring. Kept my ethics, lost the pitch. Worth it.

What I learned: Sometimes the most important design decision is knowing when not to build something. Also learned how to conduct systematic user research, synthesize conflicting stakeholder needs, and present complex technical concepts to non-technical decision-makers.

2024 - Computational gene drives: Building analysis pipelines that actually work

Part of my MRes at Imperial. Built a computational pipeline from scratch to extract and quantify guide RNA sequences from different gene drives, then fed that into a multivariate model to see if gRNA sequence scores influenced inheritance rates.

Also quantified promoter expression levels using RNAseq data to understand how timing affects gene drive success. Used Bash, R, and Python. Collaborated with my supervisor Seb via GitHub—learned proper version control, code review processes, and how to write documentation that future-me wouldn't hate.

What I learned: How to build data analysis workflows, how to debug complex multi-step pipelines, and that good documentation is a love letter to your future self. Also: scientists desperately need better tools. Every analysis required duct-taping together three different scripts in different languages. There's so much room for better developer experience in research computing.

2022 - First public health project: Designing for India's medication adherence crisis

Worked on a public health project analyzing population census data to understand what factors influence medication adherence in India. Helped with data analysis in R and contributed to the published paper (see here).

This was my first experience working with messy, real-world data at scale. Census data is gloriously chaotic—missing fields, inconsistent formatting, data entry errors. Had to design cleaning pipelines that were robust enough to handle edge cases but flexible enough to preserve legitimate outliers.

What I learned: How to work with large datasets, how to communicate statistical findings to policymakers, and that the most important question in data analysis is "what decision will this inform?"

2021 - Entheon Biomedical: Literature review + learning design collaboration

First time working for a startup. Absolutely loved it. Had to wear many different hats. My main task was to refine the product after a merger—a gene test kit for dosing psychedelics in psychedelic-assisted therapy. Conducted an in-depth literature review, presented findings to the CEOs of both companies, and my research resulted in modifications to the gene test kit.

Also worked alongside the design team on pitch deck design. My job: translate dense scientific concepts into clear visuals for investor presentations. Learned how to simplify gene networks into digestible diagrams, turn research data into scannable charts, and structure slides around single key messages. This was my first experience collaborating with a professional designer—I'd provide scientific content and rough concepts, designer would create the visual, we'd iterate for accuracy and clarity.

What I learned: How to synthesize complex scientific literature into actionable product decisions, how to communicate with stakeholders who have different priorities (science vs. business vs. regulatory), and that startups move fast—you need to be comfortable making decisions with incomplete information. Also learned that design is fundamentally about translation: making complex information accessible isn't dumbing it down, it's respecting your audience's time and cognitive load.

2020-2021 - Autism Research Centre: Only student researcher for 8 months

Worked with Varun Warrier and Armandina Almanza-Gutierrez to analyze data in R, investigating differences in masking behavior between autistic girls and autistic boys. This was my first real experience with behavioral data analysis and working in a research environment.

What I learned: How to work independently on long-term projects, how to ask good questions when stuck, and that understanding human behavior requires both quantitative rigor and qualitative empathy. You can't reduce people to data points.

2019 - Mind-controlled video game with Arduino: When EEG headsets lie

Built (or attempted to build) a mind-controlled video game using EEG probes and Arduino. Honestly can't remember if we got it fully working, but it made me deeply skeptical of consumer EEG devices.

If I needed to focus that hard to get a signal, it's not really "passive brain monitoring," is it? Made me realize that a lot of consumer neurotech is selling the aesthetic of science rather than actual functionality. EEG needs way more sensitivity before it's useful outside controlled lab settings.

What I learned: How to prototype with hardware, how to debug sensor data, and the importance of honest evaluation—sometimes the most valuable outcome is learning what doesn't work.

Somehow convinced Ticketmaster to let a 15-year-old intern with their software and design teams for the summer. Was tasked with designing and building my version of an update to their mobile app, then presenting it to the teams.

Did user research (interviewed everyone I knew about ticket-buying frustrations), designed a prototype (simplified checkout, improved event discovery, better seat visualization), built it with HTML/CSS/JavaScript, and presented to a room of professional designers and engineers. Terrifying. Also exhilarating.

What I learned: How to present design rationale, how to take critique without taking it personally, and that design decisions need to balance user needs with business constraints. Also learned that 15-year-old me's aesthetic (gradients everywhere!) was questionable, but the research process was solid.

This was the first time I realized you could combine design, code, and understanding people into an actual career. Also the first time I used Figma and understood what a design system was.

How I think about design

Start with the user's actual problem, not the solution you want to build. I killed nGENEsis because I fell in love with the science instead of the problem. With Pont, I started with "finding the right therapist is impossible" and let that drive every decision.

Build things you can actually ship. My background in biology taught me experimental design. My startup experience taught me that experiments need to end. Prototype fast, test with real users, iterate based on feedback. Don't wait for perfect.

Empathy is a skill, not a personality trait. My work with autistic kids taught me this. Understanding users isn't about "being nice"—it's about structured observation, asking good questions, and recognizing your own assumptions. Research methods matter.

Technical constraints are design constraints. I code my own prototypes because I need to know what's actually possible. When you understand how the backend works, you make smarter frontend decisions. When you understand API design, you build better developer tools.

Good design is invisible until it breaks. The best interfaces feel obvious in retrospect. Getting there requires iteration, user testing, and the humility to realize your first idea probably isn't the right one.
